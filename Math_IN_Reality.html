01 The Golden Ratio φ — Universe's Favourite Proportion geometry
▶
φ = (1 + √5) / 2 ≈ 1.6180339887… — the unique positive number satisfying φ² = φ + 1. It is the most irrational number; its continued fraction is [1; 1, 1, 1, …], converging slowest of all.
Any rectangle with side ratio φ:1 can have a square cut from it, leaving a smaller golden rectangle — infinitely. The diagonal of each nested square traces out a golden spiral, which is a logarithmic spiral with growth factor e^(2π/ln φ) per quarter turn.
The golden spiral is a complex logarithmic spiral. Under the complex log map, it straightens into a horizontal line — the same "unrolling" that 3Blue1Brown applies to Escher's Print Gallery.
Fibonacci numbers 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21… converge to φ in ratio; phyllotaxis (leaf/seed spirals in sunflowers) encodes φ to minimise overlap — nature minimising a complex-plane problem.
φ satisfies φ = 1 + 1/φ. Iterate this: start at 1, compute 1 + 1/x repeatedly. This is the fixed-point iteration for the golden ratio — a conformal contraction on the complex plane.
02 Complex Numbers — The Hidden 2D Number System complex analysis
▶
A complex number z = x + iy lives on the 2D plane. Multiplication by i rotates 90°; multiplication by re^(iθ) scales by r and rotates by θ.
Polar form: z = r·e^(iθ) where r = |z| is the modulus and θ = arg(z) is the argument. This is Euler's formula: e^(iθ) = cos θ + i·sin θ.
Image pixels are just complex numbers: pixel at (x, y) ↔ z = (x − W/2) + i(y − H/2). Every image transform is secretly a function f: ℂ → ℂ.
Holomorphic (complex-differentiable) functions preserve angles between curves at every point — they are conformal maps. They map infinitesimally small squares to squares.
The Cauchy–Riemann equations ∂u/∂x = ∂v/∂y, ∂u/∂y = −∂v/∂x are the condition for conformality. Escher intuitively satisfied these by making grid cells remain square.
03 The Complex Logarithm — Unrolling the Spiral core transform
▶
log(z) = ln|z| + i·arg(z) — the complex log maps: radial distance → horizontal position, and angle → vertical position. Concentric circles become horizontal lines. Radial rays become vertical lines.
To "take the logarithm of an image": for each output pixel (u, v), find its source via z = e^(u+iv). This pulls back the image along the exponential map — making spirals straight.
The inverse: complex exponential e^z = e^u·(cos v + i·sin v). For each output pixel, sample the input at that complex position. Rectangles wrap into annuli. Straight lines become spirals.
This is exactly how De Smit & Lenstra reverse-engineered Escher's Print Gallery: they took the logarithm of the original print, recovering the "straight" Droste-effect image underneath the conformal warp.
The logarithm is multi-valued: log(z) = ln|z| + i(θ + 2πk). Choosing a branch (e.g. θ ∈ (−π, π]) gives the principal value log. Each sheet of the Riemann surface gives a differently rotated/scaled spiral.
04 Escher's Print Gallery & the Droste Effect art + math
▶
Escher's 1956 lithograph Prentententoonstelling depicts a man viewing a print that contains the gallery containing the man — a self-referential loop. The Droste effect: the embedded image is 256× smaller (= 4⁴, one 4× zoom per corner).
The construction: start with a self-similar "straightened" image → apply a conformal mesh warp that distributes the 256× zoom factor around a loop → the warp is the complex exponential map with an appropriate scaling constant.
Mathematically: the warp is f(z) = z · e^(2πi/4) followed by exponentiation — multiplication by a complex constant in log-space corresponds to a spiral similarity (rotation + scaling) in the original plane.
The blank circle at the centre is a branch point — the logarithm is undefined at z = 0. Every conformal Droste loop must have such a singularity at the origin.
De Smit and Lenstra (2003) showed the unique "correct" completion of Escher's missing centre is another copy of the whole picture, rotated by the exact phase angle of the conformal map. The diffusion model failed because it doesn't know complex analysis.
05 Power Maps & Möbius Transforms conformal zoo
▶
z ↦ z^n: the power map wraps angle by factor n while scaling radii. It is conformal everywhere except z = 0 (critical point). For n = 2: squares the complex plane — maps semicircle to full circle.
Möbius (bilinear) transform: f(z) = (az+b)/(cz+d) with ad−bc ≠ 0. Maps circles and lines to circles and lines. All Möbius transforms form a group isomorphic to PGL(2, ℂ) — the symmetries of the Riemann sphere.
The Riemann sphere is the complex plane plus a point at infinity (∞). Under stereographic projection, Möbius transforms become rotations, reflections, and dilations of the sphere. The golden ratio appears as the cross-ratio of the vertices of an icosahedron inscribed in the sphere.
Schwarz–Christoffel formula: maps the upper half-plane to any polygon with prescribed angles. Used in cartography (conformal projections), electrostatics (boundary value problems), and fluid dynamics (wing design via Joukowski transform).
06 Why Conformal Maps Appear Everywhere in Physics deep structure
▶
Laplace's equation ∇²φ = 0 governs electrostatics, gravity, heat flow, and inviscid fluid flow. Its solutions (harmonic functions) are exactly the real/imaginary parts of holomorphic functions. Conformal maps preserve Laplace's equation — any solution in one domain transforms to a solution in a conformally equivalent domain.
2D CFT (conformal field theory) in string theory and condensed matter physics exploits an infinite-dimensional conformal symmetry group (Virasoro algebra) — the operator content of the theory is determined entirely by conformal weights.
The AdS/CFT correspondence (Maldacena duality) maps a gravitational theory in anti-de Sitter space to a conformal field theory on its boundary — the boundary is essentially the complex plane, and the bulk is its hyperbolic extension. φ appears as the self-similar scaling of this geometry.
In signal processing: the Mellin transform is the logarithm's Fourier partner. It diagonalises scale-multiplication, just as the Fourier transform diagonalises translation. Image texture analysis under scale change uses the Mellin transform — via a log-polar coordinate change, which is exactly the complex log map applied to images.
The Penrose tiling — non-periodic tiling with fivefold symmetry — has local self-similarity with scaling ratio φ. Its diffraction pattern is the Fourier transform of a quasicrystal, and its projection geometry involves the complex logarithm of the golden ratio.
